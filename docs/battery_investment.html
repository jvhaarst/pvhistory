Is the battery worth buying?

Every earlier page asked what size to buy. This one asks whether to buy at all, for hardware already chosen, and compares it against leaving the money invested elsewhere. The arithmetic is simple; three assumed rates decide the answer, and the assumed battery life decides it most of all.

Everything is nominal: a nominal alternative return discounts nominal savings that inflate, so deflating the savings as well would count inflation twice. The battery is assumed worthless after 10 years and no residual value is credited.

The verdict

A BSL B-LFP48-200PW at 10.24 kWh, with the inverter and DC hardware, costs €2,834 including VAT. Over an assumed 10 year life it loses to leaving the money invested at 10%, by €297 in today's money, and implies a return of 7.6% per year. It first pulls ahead at 12 years.

  • At the 10-year warranty: behind by €297, an implied return of 7.6%, or 8.8% once the yearly savings are reinvested at the 10% alternative rather than at the implied rate itself.
  • At the 15-year design life: ahead by €444, an implied return of 12.5%, or 11.1% once the yearly savings are reinvested at the 10% alternative rather than at the implied rate itself.

The manufacturer states both figures, and they disagree about the verdict — which is the honest headline: this purchase is decided by how long the hardware lasts, not by anything about the battery's performance. Rated cycle life is not the binding limit: at the measured cycling rate it would take about 39 years to use up the rated cycles, far beyond either calendar figure. Two returns are quoted because they answer different questions. The implied return is the rate at which this purchase breaks even, and it silently assumes each year's saving is reinvested at that same rate — which nobody can do, since if it were available it would be the alternative. The achieved return reinvests at the 10% actually on offer, and is the one to compare. Both give the same verdict here; they differ in how large the margin looks.

Year by year

The saving rises with energy prices and falls as the battery ages, and discounting pulls the later years down hardest. Degradation is applied to the capacity and then read off the measured saving curve, never used to scale the euros: the curve is concave and this battery sits past its elbow, so the first years lose very little to it.

How much the assumptions matter

Each input moved alone, the others held at their assumed values. The assumed life is on the dashed line and its own axis, because it is measured in years rather than percent — and because an earlier version of this card left it out while the page called it the input that decides the answer. The circled point on each line is the assumption in the table above. A line that crosses zero inside its plausible range is an assumption the answer genuinely depends on.

What this does not include

No residual value: the battery is assumed worthless at the end of the horizon, which is deliberately conservative — after 10 years the pack still holds about 86% of its nameplate capacity, and that is credited at nothing. Nor is anything counted that cuts the other way: the panels themselves degrade, the household's consumption will drift, and the inverter inside the hardware cost is unlikely to outlast the far end of the chart above without replacement. No maintenance, no insurance, and no subsidy. The saving itself comes from the meter-based analysis and inherits its assumptions, including that the tariff's shape holds: the entire case rests on exported energy earning about a cent while imported energy costs 18.2 to 30.5 times that.
