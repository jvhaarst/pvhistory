The fault, measured

PVOutput's consumption channel is cross-checked against the smart meter every month across the whole record. The ratio holds near 1.0 through November 2022, then steps down in December 2022 and never recovers, drifting further down year by year after the step — a discrete fault that then worsens, and the reason phase 2's battery recommendation is now superseded for any period after that step.

What a night actually costs, both ways

Night-energy distributions from the meter and from PVOutput, restricted to the period both sources cover. Where PVOutput's channel was already faulty, its curve sits visibly below the meter's — the same fault chart one shows, expressed as missed energy rather than a ratio.

The winter wall

Median daytime surplus against median night need, per month, both measured at the meter. In January, February, October, November and December the median daytime surplus is negative — the house does not send enough to the grid to cover even its own daytime draw, so a battery receives no charge at all. The worst is December, at -4.8 kWh a day against a night needing 11.2 kWh. Across the whole record only 42.9% of nights could have been covered even by an infinite battery with no power limit and no round-trip loss. That ceiling, not capacity, is what bounds this answer.

What the battery actually delivers, month by month

The same wall seen from the battery's side. Discharge at 8.5 kWh collapses in the months above that have nothing to charge from, and no capacity changes that — which is why the sizing question below is bounded by the chart above it, not by the curve.

How much capacity is worth buying, and how little the ordering matters

The meter cannot tell which of a quarter-hour's import and export happened first, so every capacity is simulated under both orderings: charge-first (8.5 kWh, the export banked before the import is served) and discharge-first (8.5 kWh, the import served before the export exists). Neither is favourable or unfavourable — within one interval charge-first bridges more, but state of charge couples the intervals, and discharging first makes room to capture export that would otherwise be spilled, so on this record discharge-first ends up marginally ahead. The two curves never separate by more than 0.06% of the grid import the house actually paid for, and they elbow at the same capacity, 8.5 kWh. The ordering the meter cannot record therefore does not change the answer — which is what running both bounds was for.

What the battery saves, in euros

Under this tariff the variable electricity bill without a battery comes to €1,322 in the most recent full year. Against the quoted hardware the optimal capacity is 8.5 kWh, saving €359 a year for a break-even installed cost of €422/kWh over 10 years. Phase 2 published a 50 kWh/yr rule of thumb that was never derived from anything and was removed at the owner's instruction; the same rule derived from these prices is 45 kWh/yr, so the guess was close but had no standing. Standing charges, network tariffs and taxes are excluded throughout: they do not change with battery size, so they cancel in every comparison. Degradation and discounting are not modelled, and payback is stated in undiscounted years.

Dropping a year with a midwinter outage moves this answer by half a step, so the exclusion is not cosmetic. The battery is modelled at 0.95 usable fraction and 0.90 round trip, both inherited from the earlier phase. Only one quoted product states a usable figure (15.27 of 16.07 kWh, i.e. 0.95); at that value the recommendation is unchanged, and at a fifteen-year horizon it rises by one to two steps.

Three terms of the €942 of non-scaling hardware are assumptions rather than quotes:

  • €19.38 — 2 m of 35 mm2 cable (assumed length)
  • €50.00 — DC isolator (assumed needed (list says optional))
  • €10.00 — VE.Bus cable (assumed (may ship with the Cerbo))

They do not move the recommendation — fixed cost is identical at every capacity and cancels in the comparison — but they shift payback by roughly a fifth of a year.

The ceiling on trading the tariff

A perfect-foresight upper bound on buying cheap and discharging dear adds at most €157 a year on top. That is a ceiling, not a policy, and it is loose in three ways that all push it up: it assumes a full extra cycle every day, ignores that the battery is already storing solar, and uses hindsight no dispatch has. It is also non-zero on only 1,254 days — the summer ones, when the battery is already full of sun. Winter's peak-to-off-peak spread is negative once the round trip is paid. Worth investigating as its own phase; not worth counting on.

Nights excluded from the analysis

Nights are dropped, not treated as low-consumption, whenever the meter has a gap inside the night window. The record has 7 gaps; 4 of them fall in January 2024, together removing most of 8–19 January — midwinter, when night consumption is at its annual peak. That removes 10 nights from this analysis around January 2024 alone.

The resolution penalty

Averaging over 15 minutes hides short peaks, so a battery simulated at that resolution might look better than reality. Rather than assume a size for this effect, it is measured directly: phase 2's own finer PVOutput data is aggregated onto the 15-minute boundaries the meter keeps, and the sweep is re-run. The measured difference is -0.011% of the 15-minute figure — below a tenth of a percent, which is the scale at which this measurement's own boundary choices move it, so it is not usefully distinguishable from zero. It is stated because it was measured, not because it is large. Near zero is what the mechanism predicts: at night there is no generation to cancel against load inside an interval, and a power cap in kW binds at the same rate however long the interval is, so coarsening can only blur short peaks and daytime charging.

How EV nights were identified

A night counts as EV-charging when its samples spend at least some hours above a power threshold. Phase 2's rule — two hours above 2 kW — is retained unchanged and applied to the meter. It was not re-derived: nothing here fits a threshold, and an earlier version of this page wrongly said otherwise. The reason it can be retained is in the table below — the split moves with the threshold, but the recommended capacity does not, so the choice does not have to be defended. It remains a heuristic, not a measurement: the meter is whole-house and the car is not sub-metered. The table below shows how the split moves as the threshold moves, republished against the meter.
